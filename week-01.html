Learning objectives

  • Distinguish first cost, acquisition cost, ownership cost, and total life-cycle cost, and explain why the split matters for software- and AI-intensive systems.
  • Describe the system life cycle (concept, development, production, operations and support, disposal) and locate where cost is committed versus where it is spent.
  • Classify an ML system by learning paradigm and identify the cost signature of each (data, training compute, inference compute, evaluation, retraining).
  • Set up the computational toolchain used all semester (Excel with the Analysis ToolPak, and Python with numpy, pandas, scikit-learn, and numpy-financial).

Lecture segments

1. The cost-commitment curve

By the end of concept design roughly 70–85% of a system's life-cycle cost is locked in even though only a few percent has been spent. We revisit this curve for AI systems, where architecture decisions (model size, hosting model, retraining cadence, data pipeline) commit cost in exactly the same way.

2. From engineering economy to engineering economics of systems

The classical curriculum (present worth, rate of return, replacement) assumes discrete, well-scoped projects. Systems engineers cost capabilities that evolve continuously. FF Chapter 1 sets out the framing we use: total ownership cost across the full life cycle, with risk treated explicitly.

3. A working taxonomy of ML techniques for cost analysts

Supervised learning (regression, classification), unsupervised (clustering, anomaly detection), reinforcement learning, and generative/foundation models each carry a different cost signature: labeled-data intensity, compute intensity at training versus inference, retraining cadence, and evaluation burden. You will need to recognize which pattern a system uses before you can build its cost element structure.

4. What makes AI systems economically different

Four properties drive the semester: (1) compute is both a capital expense and an operating expense depending on hosting choice; (2) marginal cost of an inference is non-zero and scales with usage, unlike conventional software; (3) models depreciate through data drift and competitive obsolescence, not wear; (4) data is an asset with acquisition, labeling, storage, and governance costs that do not appear on a traditional bill of materials.

Quality management lens

Quality, defined; cost of quality as a life-cycle cost element. Juran's fitness for use, Deming's view of quality as reduction of variation, and the ISO 9000 definition (degree to which inherent characteristics fulfil requirements). The cost-of-quality model (prevention, appraisal, internal failure, external failure) is a cost element structure in its own right; we carry it through every week. For an AI system, quality decomposes into data quality, model quality, and process quality, and each has a prevention cost now and a failure cost later.
