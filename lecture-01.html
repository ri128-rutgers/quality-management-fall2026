Why life-cycle cost, and why now

A defense program office I know spent under 3% of its budget in concept design and, without knowing it, committed 80% of what the system would cost over 20 years. The architecture decisions — not the spending — set the bill. Every AI system your organization buys this year is making that same commitment right now, in a slide deck, before a dollar of compute is spent.

Six things to leave with

  • Quality is fitness for use (Juran) and reduction of variation (Deming); ISO 9000 says 'degree to which characteristics fulfil requirements'.
  • Cost of quality has four buckets: prevention, appraisal, internal failure, external failure. It is a cost element structure, and we carry it all term.
  • Life-cycle cost is committed early and spent late; the commitment curve is the reason this course exists.
  • AI systems are economically different: compute is capex or opex by choice, inference has non-zero marginal cost, models depreciate by drift, data is an asset with its own cost stream.
  • Learning paradigms have cost signatures: supervised needs labels, RL needs simulation, foundation models shift cost from training to inference and evaluation.
  • Your job this term: turn quality decisions into defensible numbers a decision-maker can act on.

Segment 1

The cost-commitment curve

By the end of concept design roughly 70–85% of a system's life-cycle cost is locked in even though only a few percent has been spent. We revisit this curve for AI systems, where architecture decisions (model size, hosting model, retraining cadence, data pipeline) commit cost in exactly the same way.

Segment 2

From engineering economy to engineering economics of systems

The classical curriculum (present worth, rate of return, replacement) assumes discrete, well-scoped projects. Systems engineers cost capabilities that evolve continuously. FF Chapter 1 sets out the framing we use: total ownership cost across the full life cycle, with risk treated explicitly.

Segment 3

A working taxonomy of ML techniques for cost analysts

Supervised learning (regression, classification), unsupervised (clustering, anomaly detection), reinforcement learning, and generative/foundation models each carry a different cost signature: labeled-data intensity, compute intensity at training versus inference, retraining cadence, and evaluation burden. You will need to recognize which pattern a system uses before you can build its cost element structure.

Segment 4

What makes AI systems economically different

Four properties drive the semester: (1) compute is both a capital expense and an operating expense depending on hosting choice; (2) marginal cost of an inference is non-zero and scales with usage, unlike conventional software; (3) models depreciate through data drift and competitive obsolescence, not wear; (4) data is an asset with acquisition, labeling, storage, and governance costs that do not appear on a traditional bill of materials.

Worked example

Sorting a real bill into P-A-F

  1. Take a manufacturer's last year: inspectors' wages, calibration, scrap, rework, warranty returns, customer audits, training, test equipment.
  2. Sort each into prevention, appraisal, internal failure, external failure. The room always argues about training and audits — that argument is the lesson.
  3. Total it. Typical finding: failure costs are 2–4× prevention + appraisal, and nobody had added them up before.
  4. Ask: what prevention spend now would cut which failure line later? That question is the entire course in one sentence.

Quality management lens

Quality, defined; cost of quality as a life-cycle cost element

Juran's fitness for use, Deming's view of quality as reduction of variation, and the ISO 9000 definition (degree to which inherent characteristics fulfil requirements). The cost-of-quality model (prevention, appraisal, internal failure, external failure) is a cost element structure in its own right; we carry it through every week. For an AI system, quality decomposes into data quality, model quality, and process quality, and each has a prevention cost now and a failure cost later.
