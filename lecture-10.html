Costing the cloud, data, and machine learning

Three teams built the same assistant: one called an API, one fine-tuned, one distilled a small model and put a router in front. A year later the cheapest per request was the most expensive to own — because the quality floor moved. Today: the AI cost stack, own-versus-rent, and the economics of adaptation techniques.

Objectives

  • Build a total cost of ownership model for an ML capability under three hosting patterns: third-party API, managed cloud with rented accelerators, and owned on-premises accelerators.
  • Decompose AI cost into training, fine-tuning, evaluation, inference, data, and people, and express inference cost per unit of work (per request, per thousand tokens, per image).
  • Model accelerator economics: utilization, depreciation horizon, energy and cooling, and the breakeven utilization at which ownership beats rental.
  • Quantify the cost consequences of adaptation and efficiency techniques: prompt engineering, retrieval-augmented generation, parameter-efficient fine-tuning, full fine-tuning, distillation, quantization, model routing, and response caching.

Six things to leave with

  • The AI cost stack: training or fine-tuning, evaluation, inference, data, people. Inference scales with demand; data and evaluation recur.
  • Own, rent, or call an API: breakeven utilization is the decision variable; rental prices fall, so the breakeven moves every year.
  • Prompting, RAG, PEFT, full fine-tuning, distillation: each shifts cost between one-time development and per-inference compute, at different accuracy.
  • Quantization, batching, routing, and caching bend the marginal cost curve; accuracy is a constraint, not an afterthought.
  • A model's economic life is set by drift and competitor releases, not the tax schedule; decide it and defend it.
  • Data quality dimensions (accuracy, completeness, timeliness…) are cost drivers; poor data shows up as failure cost downstream.

Segment 1

The AI cost stack

A cost element structure for an AI capability. Training compute $= \text{accelerator-hours} \times \text{rate} / \text{utilization}$; inference compute scales with demand and model size; data and evaluation are recurring.

Segment 2

Own, rent, or call an API

Breakeven analysis with utilization as the decision variable. Sensitivity to price-per-hour decline (a negative escalation rate), reserved and committed-use discounts, and egress costs. Students source current published prices and cite them; the model, not the numbers, is what is graded.

Segment 3

The economics of adaptation techniques

A capability can be obtained by prompting a large model, adding retrieval (RAG), parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA), full fine-tuning, or distilling to a small model. Each shifts cost between one-time development, per-inference compute, data, and evaluation. Quantization and batching reduce inference cost at some accuracy cost; a router that sends easy requests to a small model and hard ones to a large model, plus a semantic cache, changes the marginal cost curve. We build the comparison as an alternatives problem with accuracy as a constraint, not an afterthought.

Segment 4

Depreciating a model

Accelerators depreciate by tax schedule; models depreciate by drift and by competitor releases. Setting an economic life for a model and treating retraining as a capital-like refresh or as O&S. Energy cost per inference and its trend.

Quality management lens

Data quality as a cost driver

Data quality dimensions (accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, validity, uniqueness) each carry acquisition and remediation cost, and poor data quality shows up as model failure cost downstream. The cost-quality frontier for a model (accuracy versus cost per inference) is the trade curve a quality manager owns.

Check yourself

Utilization at which owning beats renting is 55%. Rental prices fall 20% next year. Breakeven moves which way?

Up — you need higher utilization to justify owning.

RAG versus fine-tuning: which raises per-inference cost, which raises one-time cost?

RAG raises per-inference (more input tokens); fine-tuning raises one-time (data, compute) and can lower per-inference.

Why is a cache hit rate a quality question, not just a cost question?

Stale cached answers are external failure cost; the hit rate you accept depends on how fast the truth changes.
