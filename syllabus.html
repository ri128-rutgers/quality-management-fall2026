Course description

Quality management for engineers, taught through the economics of quality: what it costs to prevent, appraise, and fail, across the full life cycle of a system. The course integrates the quality body of knowledge (Juran and Deming, cost of quality, Taguchi loss, process capability and statistical process control, FMEA, DMAIC, supplier quality, ISO 9001 and ISO/IEC 42001, NIST AI RMF) with economic analysis of engineering alternatives across the full system life cycle, with emphasis on software-, cloud-, data-, and AI-intensive systems. Time value of money, comparison of alternatives, depreciation and after-tax analysis, inflation, cost estimating relationships, machine-learned CERs, and learning curves, life-cycle and total-ownership-cost frameworks, simulation-based (Monte Carlo) costing with Bayesian updating and conformal intervals, costing of complex and software-intensive systems, cloud and machine-learning cost models including the economics of fine-tuning, retrieval augmentation, distillation, quantization, and model routing, decision and risk analysis including real options and Markov decision processes for retraining policy, off-the-shelf and foundation-model acquisition economics, earned value, benefit-cost analysis, AI assurance and governance costs, and energy economics of compute. 3 credits. Fully asynchronous.

Prerequisites. Graduate standing in engineering or permission of the instructor. An undergraduate engineering economics course (e.g., 14:540:343) is assumed; Weeks 2–3 review it at speed. Working familiarity with Excel and introductory Python.

Learning outcomes

On completing the course you will be able to:

  1. Apply the cost-of-quality model, Taguchi loss, process capability, FMEA, and DMAIC to a software- or AI-intensive system, and place quality activities in a cost element structure.
  2. Construct cash-flow models and evaluate engineering alternatives by worth, rate-of-return, and benefit-cost methods, including after-tax and inflation-adjusted analysis.
  3. Build a cost element structure and life-cycle cost estimate for a complex system, selecting estimating methods appropriate to program phase, including machine-learned cost estimating relationships validated by cross-validation and explained with SHAP, and defending them against GAO cost-estimating criteria.
  4. Quantify cost uncertainty with Monte Carlo simulation, Bayesian updating, and conformal prediction; report confidence levels; and identify the drivers that warrant better data.
  5. Estimate software and machine-learning development and sustainment cost across the MLOps lifecycle, and build a total-cost-of-ownership model comparing API, rented-cloud, and owned-accelerator hosting patterns and adaptation techniques (prompting, RAG, fine-tuning, distillation, quantization, routing).
  6. Structure investment decisions under uncertainty with decision trees, value of information, real options, Markov decision processes (retraining under drift), and multi-attribute methods.
  7. Communicate an economic analysis to executive and oversight audiences in a written report and a recorded briefing.

Software

Nothing to install. Every scaffold is a notebook that opens with one click in Google Colab (free; a Rutgers or personal Google account) and also runs in Azure Machine Learning Studio notebooks or any Jupyter. Data files load from the course repository automatically. Excel is optional: the data packs are also provided as .xlsx for anyone who prefers a spreadsheet, and memos may include spreadsheet tables. An AI assistant of your choice (Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini) is expected for drafting and is governed by the policy below.

Seat time

The course is built for three hours a week: about 45 minutes of recorded segments, an hour of reading limited to the sections listed on the module page, and 75 minutes of work (discussion, notebook, or progress on a case). Weeks with a case due trade the reading hour for the memo. Optional readings are marked optional and are never examined.

Weekly rhythm

This section opens Thursday, September 3. Modules open Thursdays at 9:00 AM ET on this site. The optional Zoom session is Thursdays at 8:00 PM ET (recorded; attendance not required). All deliverables for the week are due Wednesday at 11:59 PM ET except where the schedule says otherwise (midterm close, final project). Peer replies for discussions are due the following Friday at 11:59 PM ET so that everyone has something to reply to.

Late work

Cases and the lab: a 10% deduction per calendar day, up to five days, after which the work is not accepted. Discussion posts are not accepted late (the two lowest are dropped for this reason). The midterm window and final project deadline are firm except for documented emergencies or accommodations arranged in advance.

Final project

A life-cycle cost analysis of an AI-enabled system of your choosing (from your workplace, anonymized if needed, or from a provided list), comparing at least three acquisition/hosting alternatives, with a Monte Carlo treatment of uncertainty, a decision recommendation, and a 12-minute recorded executive briefing. Full specification on the final project page. Deliverables: proposal (Oct 21), interim report (Nov 25), final report and briefing (Dec 18). At least one alternative must be agentic or differ in ML technique; an AI-use appendix is required.

Use of generative and agentic AI

This course expects you to use AI assistants and agents the way a 2026 analyst does: to draft spreadsheets, notebooks, and prose scaffolds fast, and then to verify every number, assumption, and rule before you put your name on it. Rules: (a) each submission carries a short AI-use note stating what the tool did, what it got wrong, and what you verified; (b) analysis, judgment, and recommendations must be yours; (c) you must be able to explain and defend every line of every model you submit; (d) the midterm restricts AI use to software syntax only. The Simulation lab and each week's "Agentic AI corner" build the verification habit deliberately. Agents are also an object of study: Weeks 8, 10, 11, 12, and 14 cost agentic systems, and Case 4 governs one. Undisclosed use is an academic-integrity violation under the Rutgers policy.
